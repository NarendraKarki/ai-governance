# Source register - Sultanate of Oman AI policy set

Every document referred to, used, or deliberately not used in producing the Oman AI
Acceptable Use Policy v1.0, the Enterprise Annex v1.0, and the research note.

State as at 3 September 2026. Hashes are SHA-256 of the file as supplied, truncated to
16 characters.

---

## 0. Read this first

**Oman is among the best-resourced jurisdictions in this series**: a **comprehensive
data protection law, its executive regulations, and a national AI policy** - all held in
full, all current, none of it pending commencement.

Two consequences follow. The policy set states AI obligations that are **actually AI
obligations**, not general data protection duties applied to AI by analogy. And the set
contains **no commencement-readiness register**, because there is nothing left to be ready
for: the one-year conformity deadline under the Executive Regulations expired in early
2025.

**Both language versions are held.** The Arabic is the enacted text and therefore the
authority. **MTCIT's official English texts of the Law, the Executive Regulations and the
AI Policy are also held**, and every substantive assertion in this set has been checked
against them. Terminology throughout now follows MTCIT's own English. Section 4 records
what the check found, including where the two versions differ.

**Not used because its scope section states that it applies to the units of the State's

It was nonetheless read in full, and research note section 5 summarises it - for three

- **breach notification goes to the Electronic Defence Centre**, not to the Ministry's

- **data localisation is the default**, with processing required inside Oman's geographic

Recording a held-but-unused instrument, and saying precisely why, is how a set avoids the
failure of quietly importing a stricter regime that does not apply.

## 3. NOT HELD - the gaps that shape what these documents can say

### 3.1 The sector regulator's issuances - the standing action

**This is the most important gap, and it is structural rather than incidental.**

The national AI policy provides that regulatory authorities **align its provisions with
their own regulatory documents** and circulate them to the institutions they supervise,
and that they **may lay down additional provisions regulating the use of AI technologies
and algorithms** with the ministry's approval.

It then provides that **the regulatory authorities monitor the compliance of the
institutions in the sectors they supervise**, while the issuing ministry monitors
government units and reports to the Council of Ministers.

**So for a private-sector organisation, the body that enforces the national AI policy is
its own sector regulator, and that regulator may have added to it.** None of those
issuances is held. Policy 4.3 and 22.2, and annex A15.5, say so and make obtaining them a
standing committee action.

### 3.2 Instruments the AI policy itself names as related

The national AI policy lists eight related issuances. **Three are held** (rows 1 and 2 of
section 1, and the government-sector policy at section 2). **Five are not:**

| **IT Risk Management Framework (2014)** | Risk assessment methodology |

| **National Records Law (52/2024)** | Records and retention obligations that may qualify the erasure right |

## 4. Language, and what it means for reliance

**The Arabic governs.** It is the enacted text published in the Official Gazette; the
English is MTCIT's own rendering of it. Where the two differ, the Arabic is the law. The
English is therefore a **checking instrument, not a substitute source**, and that is how it
has been used.

### 4.1 How the set was built, and how it was then checked

The set was **written from the Arabic**. The Law and the Regulations carry a text layer
that extracts in a legacy encoding and comes out garbled; the AI policy and the circular
are image-only with no text layer at all. All four were therefore **read as rendered
pages**, which is more reliable than trusting a broken encoding but leaves no text to grep.

**That is why no machine-checkable verification was possible at first**, and why the
initial checks on this set were limited to hygiene checks rather than verbatim quotation
checks.

The official English texts were obtained afterwards. They are also image-only, so they were
**OCR'd at 200-300 dpi with Tesseract** to produce a searchable layer, and every
substantive assertion in the set was then checked against it - the gap that had been
recorded as this set's principal weakness.

### 4.2 What the check confirmed

Every material figure, period and trigger holds:

- **both 72-hour breach clocks** and their differing triggers - to the Competent Department
  "if it poses a risk to the rights of the personal data subjects", and to the individual
  "if such breach would result in serious harm or high risks";
- **every penalty band**, including the decisive one: **OMR 100,000 to 500,000** for the
  transfer contravention against a maximum of OMR 20,000 for anything else;
- the **permit timetable** - 45 days, reasoned refusal, 60-day grievance, 30-day answer,
  five-year term, 15-day amendment notice;
- the **complaints timetable** - 30 / 7 / 14 / 60 days, and the 60-day grievance with a
  30-day answer;
- the **45-day free-of-charge** period for a data subject request, and the **60-day**
  external auditor report;
- the **transfer conditions**, including both exemptions from consent;
- the **AI policy scope**, its use and development controls, and its ethical principles.

**Nothing substantive was found to be wrong.**

### 4.3 What the check changed

**Terminology.** The set now uses MTCIT's own English throughout:

| Was (working translation) | Now (MTCIT English) |
|---|---|
| Competent Administration | **Competent Department** |
| express consent | **explicit consent** |
| higher interests of the State | **supreme interests of the state** |
| distinctive marks or explanatory notices | **labelling or explanatory notices** |
| transparency, integrity and respect for human dignity | **transparency, honesty and respect for human dignity** |

**A substantive correction**, recorded at section 9.1: the Arabic distribution list of the
AI policy is narrower than its own scope section, and the set had followed the scope. **The
English distribution list explicitly includes the private sector**, confirming that reading
on the very page that had appeared to contradict it.

### 4.4 Divergences worth knowing

- **The AI policy's English title** is *Public Policy for Safe and Ethical Use of
  Artificial Intelligence Systems*, not "General Policy for the Safe and Ethical Use of..."
- **The English Law and Regulations are not perfectly consistent with each other.** Law
  Art 10 renders the consent requirement as "explicit **approval**" and requires the
  controller to "**provide proof** of the written approval", while the Regulations use
  "explicit **consent**" throughout. The set uses "explicit consent", which is the
  Regulations' term and the more common one.
- **Regulations Art 31(2)**, where the regulator may involve itself in notifying
  individuals, reads "**Requesting** to notify the personal data subject" in English,
  against a stronger Arabic formulation closer to *directing*. The set follows the Arabic,
  since the Arabic governs, but an organisation should expect the softer English wording in
  correspondence.

### 4.5 What the check did not do

**It is still not a dual extraction of the Arabic** - two independent extractions
agreeing with each other. Checking an Arabic-derived reading against an official English
translation is a strong cross-check, but it is not that. Research note section 10 states
what would have to happen first before these provisions were derived into structured,
machine-readable rules.

## 9. The verifications that mattered most

### 9.1 The scope of the AI policy - and a divergence inside one document

The **Arabic** document-control page carries a distribution list reading only **"all units
of the State's administrative apparatus"**. Its **scope section** states that it applies to
**"all government units and private-sector institutions subject to regulation by the
regulatory authorities"** which develop or use AI systems, across implementation, data
collection, system design, training, operation and continuous evaluation. Its
**definitions** confirm this, defining both the *using entity* and the *developing entity*
as "any **governmental or private** institution".

**A reader who saw only the Arabic distribution list would conclude this is a
government-only policy** - and would then write no Oman AI policy at all for a
private-sector client, or would write one that disclaimed the very instrument it should be
built on.

**The official English text settles it.** Its distribution list reads **"All State
Administrative Apparatus units and the Private Sector"**, and its scope section reads
"This document applies to all government administrative units and private sector
organizations regulated by the relevant regulatory authorities that are involved in the
development or use of AI systems." In English the distribution list and the scope section
agree; the divergence exists only in the Arabic.

The set followed the scope provision before the English was held, records the divergence
rather than silently resolving it, and captures the real qualification: the private-sector
reach runs **through sector regulation**, which is also why the sector regulator is the
enforcement channel.

### 9.2 Two breach clocks, both 72 hours, with different triggers

Regulations **Art 30** requires notification to the Competent Department **within 72
hours of the controller's knowledge**, where the breach **would lead to a risk threatening
the rights of data subjects**. Regulations **Art 32** requires notification to the **data
subject within 72 hours of the controller's knowledge**, where the breach **would cause
serious damage or high risks to that data subject**.

**Same period, same starting point, different tests.** A breach can cross one threshold
and not the other.

The obvious drafting error - and the one this set was written to avoid - is a policy that
states "notify within 72 hours" once and lets the reader assume a single duty. Policy 15.4
requires a recorded determination for **each** limb; annex A9.2 makes a simulated breach
crossing one but not the other the specific internal audit test; and annex A10.2 requires
the two intervals to be reported separately and **never as an average**, because an average
conceals exactly that failure.

Regulations **Art 31(2)** adds a third element the policy plans for: **the regulator may
direct that data subjects be notified**, after the organisation has decided not to.

### 9.3 The penalty structure, and one figure that dominates it

Reading Law Arts 25 to 32 together produces a table that is not ordered as one would
expect, and one band that dwarfs the rest.

**Contravention of the cross-border transfer provision carries a fine of OMR 100,000 to
500,000. Every other contravention in the statute tops out at OMR 20,000.**

Stated precisely: **the minimum fine for a transfer contravention is five times the
maximum fine available for anything else in the Law.**

That single comparison reorganised the policy. Processing location became a **gating
question at tool approval** rather than a diligence detail (policy 3.2, 17.8); Section 17
is the longest operational section in the document; the annex gives transfers their own
register and their own committee discipline (A6); and no exception may be granted against
the transfer prohibition (A13.2).

A second observation from the same table, recorded because it is counter-intuitive:
**failing to establish the controls and procedures for processing (Art 13) is priced above
failing to appoint the data protection officer or to retain processing documents (Art
26)**. The architecture is treated as more serious than the roles.

### 9.4 The permit is a licence with a lead time, and tooling changes amend it

Law Art 5 prohibits processing the sensitive categories **without a permit from the
Ministry**. Regulations Arts 5 to 10 make it a licence rather than a notification: **up to
45 days** to decide, **a reasoned refusal**, **silence deemed a rejection**, a **60-day**
grievance to the Minister with **30 days** for an answer before that too is deemed
refused, a term of **up to five years**, and **cancellation** for, among other things,
failing to notify amendments.

**The finding that changes operational practice** is that four of the eight application
fields are facts about tooling - the processor, the recipients of disclosure, the places of
transfer or storage, and the protection systems. So **changing an AI supplier, adding a
sub-processor, or moving a workload between regions changes the permit**, engages the
**15-day** amendment duty at Reg Art 9, and failure to notify is a cancellation ground at
Reg Art 10(3).

Policy 11.5 and annex A4.4 therefore screen every inventory-reopening event against the
Permit Register, and annex A4.3 starts renewal six months before expiry because a lapsed
permit stops the processing.

### 9.5 The shortest deadline in the framework is not one of the 72-hour clocks

Regulations Art 42 gives the controller **14 days from being notified** to reply to a
complaint forwarded by the Competent Department. That is shorter than any other
externally imposed period in the framework, and it arrives without warning.

Annex A12.2 therefore requires a standing process and a named owner, so that a complaint
arriving during a holiday period does not consume it.
