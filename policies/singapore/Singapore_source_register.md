## 5. Document architecture

[ORGANISATION] supplied an existing policy set as a structural template. Only its
architecture was used: section order, tone, the convention that section numbers live
in the research note rather than the policy body, and the practice of flagging house
standards that exceed statute.

**No legal source from that template was carried across.** Every obligation in this
set was derived independently from the instrument in section 1. Where the template
carried a citation and no equivalent Singapore provision exists, the clause here is an
unsourced house standard and is labelled as such in the policy text itself, not only
in this register.

## 8. The verification that mattered most

Three. The first two pull in opposite directions; the third was nearly missed.

**The first is an absence.** A full-text search of the enacted Act returns no
occurrence of "artificial intelligence", "automated decision", "profiling", or
"algorithm". It would have been easy - and wrong - to write a Singapore AI policy
around obligations imported from elsewhere: a prohibited-practice list, a risk
classification, a right to an explanation. None of that is in this statute. Every such
clause in the policy is labelled a house standard **in the policy text**, where a
reader will see it, not only in this register.

**The second is a presence that is easy to miss.** ss.48D, 48E and 48F make an
individual personally liable, with imprisonment available, for disclosing or using
personal data without the organisation's authorisation, or for re-identifying
anonymised information. The offences turn on the word *authorised*, and the Act leaves
the organisation to define it. That converts an acceptable use policy from an internal
control document into the instrument that sets the boundary of an individual's
criminal exposure.

That is why policy Section 11 exists, why the approved tools list at policy Section 3
is described as the boundary of authorisation, and why annex A7.3 requires every
exception to state precisely whom and what it covers.

**The third was a correction to a draft.** An earlier draft of policy 9.3 stated that
AI-inferred data about a person is subject to every obligation "including access and
correction". Reading the Fifth and Sixth Schedules against each other showed that to
be wrong: **derived personal data is excepted from the correction right at Sixth
Schedule para 1(f) and is not excepted from the access right at all.** The clause was
rewritten. Research note 4.4 sets out the position and why [ORGANISATION] declines to
rely on the exception.

The general lesson is worth stating, because it applies beyond this set: **the
provision that decides an AI question was not in a section about decisions or about
data quality. It was one line in a schedule of exceptions.** Reading the operative
sections and stopping there would have produced a policy that was wrong in the
direction of overpromising - which is the direction that gets an organisation into
difficulty when it cannot deliver what its own policy said it would.
